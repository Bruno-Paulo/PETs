# Synthetic Data

Synthetic data refers to the generation of data that retains the data
patterns and statistical properties of the original dataset without
disseminating the actual data. Synthetic Data serves as an output
privacy technique, safeguarding sensitive datasets by transforming them
into new datasets with comparable statistical properties. It aims at
ensuring both privacy and utility for statistical analysis [[1]](#1).
Synthetic data can be *fully synthetic*, meaning that it is entirely
generated by a model, *partially synthetic*, where only some variables
are generated, or *hybrid* where it combines real and fully synthetic
data, aiming to maintain usefulness for specific purposes. Hybrid
synthetic data is typically employed in medical research while
preserving statistical properties similar to the original data
[[2]](#2).

## Current and potential applications

Synthetic data ensures privacy protection for sensitive data and
maintains the utility of the dataset for analysis purposes [[1]](#1).
It finds applications in various domains, such as training AI models,
testing software, sharing data for official statistics, and generating
synthetic digital content, where preserving overall statistical
properties is crucial for achieving outcomes similar to the original
data source [[2]](#2). Where sufficient data is not available, synthetic
data can also be used to extend or improve existing datasets
[[3]](#3). Synthetic data is also often used to train AI
models when access to large datasets is restricted or too expensive
[[4]](#4).

## Efficiency and parameters

Synthetic data generation algorithms typically employ machine learning
models, known as the ‘model-based approach’, which uses advanced
inference and deep learning techniques to learn the distribution of the
original input data. This enables the creation of new data records that
reflect the overall characteristics of the original dataset [[1]](#1).
Some quality metrics are used to evaluate the quality of synthetic data,
such as *fidelity*, which measures how realistic the synthetic data is
compared to the real data, and *diversity*, which measures how well the
generated samples cover the variability of the real data [[5]](#5).

In terms of privacy, the risk associated with synthetic data can be
assessed using differential privacy, which will be discussed later. The
level of randomness introduced during the training phase, known as its
parameterisation, determines the level of privacy guarantees inherited
by the synthetic dataset. To ensure robust privacy protection, it is
crucial to track privacy loss and reset budgets during model training.
Skipping this step increases the likelihood that the model will
replicate outliers or distinct data regions, thereby compromising
privacy [[3]](#3).

## Primitives and Algorithms

The creation of synthetic data involves statistical and machine-learning
models rather than cryptographic primitives. Model-based approaches use
techniques such as *generative-adversarial networks* (GANs) [[6]](#6) and
*variational auto-encoders* (VAEs) [[7]](#7). GANs consist of two neural
networks, a *generator* and a *discriminator*, engaged in a
game-theoretic scenario where the generator generates data samples and
the discriminator evaluates their authenticity. GANs excel at generating
highly realistic data, particularly in image synthesis, but suffer from
training instability and high computational costs. GAN often perform
poorly on tabular data because real data typically consists of mixed
data types and unlike image data, which follows a Gaussian-like
distribution and can be normalised, tabular data often has non-Gaussian
distributions with long tails, leading to gradient saturation, and
continuous and unbalanced categorical data types in tabular data require
pre-processing before being fed into the generators [[5]](#5). VAEs
encode data into a latent space and decode it back to generate new
samples, optimising the variational lower bound. They are more stable
than GANs and provide smooth latent space representations, making them
suitable for generating continuous data; however, they often produce
fuzzy outputs due to the inherent trade-off in their loss function.

## Limitations

The following limitations underline the need for careful consideration
and evaluation when using synthetic data in different applications
[[1]](#1)[[2]](#2)[[3]](#3)[[4]](#4)[[8]](#8)[[9]](#9):

- **Potential bias:** Synthetic data may inherit biases present in the
  original dataset.

- **Vulnerable to re-identification:** Synthetic data is susceptible to
  re-identification attacks such as model inversion, membership
  inference, memorisation, and attribute disclosure risks.

- **Loss of utility:** The process of suppressing outliers and
  integrating synthetic data with differential privacy measures can lead
  to reduced utility, generalisation and increased unpredictability.

- **Lack of privacy guarantees:** Synthetic data itself does not
  inherently guarantee privacy. The flexibility of the model used can
  impact the sensitivity of information, potentially leading to
  over-fitting and re-identification risks.
